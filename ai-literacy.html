We’ve got to get AI literacy figured out

November 10, 2026

AI literacy is lacking and everyone is just playing catch-up. I had two distinct AI-related experiences that have been cycling in my thoughts for the past couple of weeks, first presenting at the AAC&U Conference on AI and Higher Education and then with my 17 year old nephew attending high school as an online student (and back off on online education, if traditional schooling worked for you that doesn’t mean it works for everyone). There are many observations in my notebook about what AI literacy meant to different people at the conference, and then my nephew, and I think we have difficulty generalizing and extrapolating beyond our own experiences with AI. This is common with new technologies, actually, and I feel odd that I hadn’t fully sensed it from my interactions before, but I did write about it in my June 23rd post “Boxes and Boxes”. But being able to spend 3 days discussing AI governance and policy, integration into curriculum, impacts on student learning, and more with folks who actually wanted to talk about it, well that just dumped data on me and my assumptions. All of this left me wondering how limited I have become with my own definition of AI literacy.

My simplified definition of AI literacy is a functional set of skills that allow a person to use AI tools to enhance their critical and analytical thinking, problem-solving, communication/storytelling, and to expand their ability to create. Oh yeah, and know when to not use AI.

It’s not perfect, and it never will be, and the real version is currently 2 pages long, so this is my attempt to commit to something on the topic. And that’s why I felt that there was such a disconnect between the conversations I had about AI and the work I see being done about AI. There was a lot of talking about AI, but very little discussion of applications beyond niche personal use cases. Most of those use cases leveraged one or two AI skills/agents, and rarely demonstrated an understanding of AI utility beyond the use case. And I don’t know what we should be generalizing from single projects alone, other than comparing against our societal-level definition of AI literacy. Or our personal definitions, I guess, but I’m really trying to move beyond AI frameworks built around just a handful of experiences represented in the steering committee.

I spoke with dozens of people who are in leadership positions that are at least in some way responsible for AI at their institutions, every AI-related vendor, and a lot of faculty/grad students just trying to find their way with AI and from these conversations it became clear to me that we are still all talking about a different version of AI almost every time.

Or maybe I’m just venting because I keep pushing myself to build more with AI and find my limitations, ignorances, and deficiencies. And I also do it because it’s fun for me. I’ve always grappled with task avoidance, or some executive functioning issues when starting a project and then hitting a point that would require a whole new skill or skill set just to proceed the way I would like. And maybe I should have developed that skill. But let me be clear, I’m never going to use Excel enough to need to memorize the formulas for some of the things I do as one-offs. Just not going to need it. I’d rather learn something else. I don’t know if I’ll ever make another fake radio show like The Dimming, so learning how to do all the constituent parts of that project would have been rewarding, but it wasn’t what I was setting out to learn.

There is strength in how a GenAI tool can help a leader in their daily tasks, but frontier LLMs are getting better at “remembering” things about users and what they like/dislike, tone/style preferences, etc. In my case, within my Aegis system, I know that Bubo (my hub persona) will adapt to my language without me asking for it to do so. Essentially, we’ve developed our own jargon, as many relationships do across enough communication. This happens to avid readers of a single author as well, and I experienced this as a teenager who read every Stephen King paperback my father had, which led to me speaking like a coked-up madman afraid of everything. Yes, I did very well in high school debate.

And that’s probably what is so hard about us trying to talk about AI for our institutions or departments. That we just keep thinking about our own uses and motivations, like I just did over the last three paragraphs. We have to get out of our AI towers (Silica towers? Generated towers? We need a new ivory tower meme), and get comfortable with not knowing the limits of AI in education right now. Most places aren’t really equipped for this at a structural/policy level, beyond some “don’t” boundaries. Don’t use AI detection tools, don’t violate data governance policy, don’t use unapproved tools, don’t make us look bad. All necessary requirements, honestly, I don’t disagree with any of them. But we need some “how” in this that acknowledges individual understanding of these tools and that faculty, staff, and students might be creating something brand new with AI that is responsible, fair, and compliant but maybe outside of the scope of what our policies and frameworks anticipated.

I write this because my nephew shared experiences of never knowing how to use it responsibly because there wasn’t consistency from teacher to teacher, grader to grader, assignment to assignment. He worries he may have stepped over the line of their expectations at times, but even at his retail job he is getting asked by older leaders how to use AI to make things more efficient there. He is being pulled in two directions by different worlds, one that is having difficulty telling him what he needs to know about AI and when/how to use or avoid it, and another that expects him to be able to creatively problem solve with AI. And because he is very intelligent, he gets stuck cycling between those two pulls, but decided that developing AI literacy is more important simply because he wants to be able to get a job and have a family. Left unguided, he’s going to pick the option with the most immediate impact.

I didn’t debate him about any of this, it’s his experience. But he knows what I expect a high school graduate to know, and is curious, thoughtful, creative, and a very deep critical thinker. As part of the COVID generation of young learners, his educational experience has been something we’ve never seen before, and hopefully we’ll never see again. The least we can do, those of us in education with the expertise, is come up with a definition of AI literacy and create both policy and support for how to integrate AI into the curriculum (not all aspects) toward meeting all of our goals, not just the AI literacy stuff.
