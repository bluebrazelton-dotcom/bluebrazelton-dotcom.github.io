How I build talks with AI

The standing account of how an AI agent helps me plan and build presentations, what I check, and what stays mine. Individual talks are listed in the talk log at the bottom.

In 100 words

I build my talks with an AI presentation agent I designed. I give it a written design standard and the accepted proposal. It interviews me, then drafts the message, the storyboard, the slides, the speaker notes, and the take-home pages, often in scheduled sessions while I am not there. It cannot create or change a file without my explicit yes. Every number on a slide is traced to its source before the deck is final. The argument, the research, the examples, the humor, every cut, and everything I say in the room are mine. The agent runs on Claude (Anthropic).

The process every talk follows

Each talk moves through the same eight stages, and each stage has a written definition of done. The agent works from a playbook I wrote that sets what a good talk looks like: one clear message, a full-sentence headline on every slide, interaction that asks people to think rather than fill time, humor placed on purpose and never at anyone's expense, and accessibility built in from the start. Prep dates become scheduled sessions, so much of the drafting happens while I am teaching or away from my desk.

Who does what at each stage

Three rules that hold every time

  • No file changes without my yes. The agent shows any change in full and waits for explicit approval before it writes, creates, or changes anything.
  • Dates become scheduled sessions. Prep commitments are scheduled sessions I can see and cancel, not lines in a document.
  • Unattended work stops at a proposal. When a session runs and I am not there, the agent drafts, lists what it inferred and what only I can answer, and stops. I treat that work as a proposal until I approve it.

What I always check

  • Every number on a slide. A separate research pass traces each one to its source and marks it verified, corrected, or cut before the deck is final. A flagged number is not a resolved number.
  • Institutional and policy references, against the current public source, including the URL.
  • The proposal's promises. An accepted proposal is a promise to the audience, so the talk is checked against it; where the talk departs from it, that is a decision I made and can name.
  • The deck, by checklist. Speakers are poor judges of their own slides, so review runs against a written checklist rather than my impression, and through an accessibility check against the organizer's guidance and my own accessibility standard.
  • Demos. Any recorded demo has to show what the slide before it promises, and I edit recordings for privacy before they are shown.
  • Every draft. I read everything the agent produces and change what I disagree with. Plenty of its proposals get cut.

What is always mine, and what is the agent's

Mine

  • The argument and its stakes
  • The research a talk reports; the agent builds slides from work that already exists and does not produce findings
  • The examples and stories, drawn from my own work
  • Every joke
  • Every approval and every cut
  • The verification standard the agent has to meet
  • Rehearsal, delivery, and every answer to a question

The agent's

  • First drafts of the message, storyboard, slides, and speaker notes
  • First drafts of take-home pages and handouts
  • Scheduling the prep sessions
  • The checklist review
  • All of it under the three rules above

Data limits

  • No protected data. Nothing containing student data, internal institutional data, or personal data about anyone else is given to the agent for talk work.
  • Narrow access. The agent can reach one project folder, not my whole drive or my inbox.
  • The right tool for the data. My research, writing, and talk preparation run on a personal account with no institutional data in it. Anything specific to Northern Arizona University runs in the tool NAU licenses.
  • A human says yes. The agent drafts; nothing is written, sent, or deleted until I approve it.

Tools

  • Claude (Anthropic), in its desktop application, runs the agent and its scheduled sessions.
  • PowerPoint for every deck, on the organizer's template when there is one and on my own otherwise.
  • DidaRec, a browser screen recorder I built, for demo recordings.

The inclusion of any tool here is not an endorsement or approval by Northern Arizona University; approval for use with NAU data varies by department and context.

Beyond Compliance: Reading AI Governance as a Signal of Institutional Digital Maturity

AAC&U Conference on AI and Higher Education, online. Thursday, October 29, 2026. Theory to Practice session, 45 minutes; Policy, Governance, and Ethics track. Upcoming

How it was built
I finished the intake in a live interview on October 7. The agent drafted the message, the arc, and the storyboard in a scheduled session, and I approved them on October 12 with changes, including how the talk would handle a gap between what the proposal described and what the analysis reports. The agent built the deck and the six-indicator self-assessment handout unattended on October 15 and showed them to me with seven departures from the storyboard listed and explained; I approved them on October 16. On October 22 the agent moved the approved deck onto my personal slide template with larger type minimums. The content didn't change, but six headlines were shortened and two slides were split to fit the larger type. I approved them the same day.
What was checked
Two separate verification passes, on October 12 and October 15, checked the slide figures against the study manuscript, the quotations from Kane et al. (2019) against the book, and the details of the one institution named as an example against its current public pages. One slide headline was corrected: the ratio it reported holds across the full sample, not within each type of institution. The self-assessment handout is also a tagged, accessible PDF.
What was mine
The research the talk reports: my study of AI governance frameworks at 400 colleges and universities (under review). The decision to name no institution as a poor example, since the session is recorded and some of the people who wrote those frameworks may be in the room. The examples, the discussion questions I chose to keep, and the humor.

Reclaim Your Time, Reclaim Your Agency: AI as Productivity Infrastructure for the Professoriate

AAC&U Conference on AI and Higher Education, online. Thursday, October 29, 2026. Innovation session, 20 minutes including discussion; The Future of Work, the Professoriate, and Higher Education track. Upcoming

How it was built
A scheduled session pre-filled the intake from the accepted proposal on October 12, and I finished it in a live interview the same day. The agent drafted the message, the storyboard, and the interaction plan in a scheduled session on October 17, and I approved them that day. On October 22 the agent built the deck on my personal slide template and the one-page starter-harness handout in a live session with me, and listed ten departures from the storyboard with a reason for each for my review. Parts of the first Tech Summit talk were reused and rebuilt for this conference's type sizes: the one-line disclosure, the system diagram, and the verification-audit appendix.
What was different
This talk is about the same agent system that helped build it. Its examples of what that system does come from the system's own files and records, and the talk states its limits plainly, including a small verification audit (six test prompts, 18 transcripts) in which the specialized personas only partly outperformed the base model. The demo is a recording of my real system, privacy-edited by me, not a live run.
What was checked
A verification pass on October 22 checked every number and checkable claim on the deck and the handout. Two held as written: the scheduled-task figures and the verification-audit result. Four were corrected. The workload research no longer says faculty workload is “growing,” because none of the three studies measures a trend, and one citation's authorship was fixed. Two comparable tools from other vendors are listed under their new names, since both are being renamed this fall. My institution's data rule is described as guidance rather than a ban, because the limit depends on the tool. The handout is also a tagged, accessible PDF.
What was mine
The origin story. The examples from my own teaching, research, service, and assessment work. The four privacy rules the talk teaches. The humor.
