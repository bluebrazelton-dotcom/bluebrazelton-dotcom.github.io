Welcome Aboard: The AI Industry Just Became an Airline

November 24, 2026

Last Thursday, Anthropic started trading on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT. The company sold 956 million shares at $83 apiece, and with a private placement on top, the total haul came to more than $100 billion, the largest IPO on record. Plenty of people will spend this week arguing about the stock. Boarding has begun, and I want to talk about something else: the price of the ticket.

For three years, most of us have been using frontier AI the way you use a hotel breakfast buffet. You pay one price, you load up the plate, and nobody at the omelet station counts your eggs. That era was paid for by private investors who were happy to lose money while the industry grew up. Public markets are a different kind of investor. They want a quarterly report, and they want the line at the bottom to be black.

So here is my organizing metaphor for the week, borrowed with credit from Citadel Securities strategist Frank Flight: AI companies are turning into airlines.

The S-1 reads like a fuel bill

Anthropic’s prospectus is worth skimming even if you never plan to buy a share. Revenue in the first half of 2026 was $16.3 billion, up more than 1,300 percent from a year earlier. Compute in those same six months cost about $8.9 billion, more than the company spent on compute in all of 2025. The number that stuck with me sits further down: roughly $900 billion in commitments to compute and infrastructure, stretching well into the next decade.

That is an airline’s balance sheet. Nearly a trillion dollars in commitments makes individual consumer problems less than a rounding error. Revenue is growing fast, but the fuel and the planes are bought years in advance, and the margin depends on filling every seat at the right price. OpenAI, which filed its own confidential paperwork in June, has told investors it does not expect to turn a profit until 2029 or 2030. Every frontier lab is now flying the same route.

Basic economy and the front of the cabin

Here is the strange part. Depending on which chart you look at, AI is either getting much cheaper or much more expensive. Both are true.

At the bottom of the market, prices are in free fall. DeepSeek released a model in September at fifteen cents per million input tokens off-peak. Flight’s analysis found that consumption-weighted token prices dropped about 50 percent in six months, largely because cheaper open-weight models now carry so much of the traffic. That’s basic economy. You get where you’re going, and nobody brings you a snack (though with the way robotics are progressing, maybe the ClaudeBot premium model will have a pretzel compartment).

At the top, prices have been climbing. Earlier this year, new flagship models from OpenAI and Anthropic launched at roughly double the per-token price of the models they replaced. Faster responses cost a multiple of standard pricing, and long context and data residency carry surcharges. If you have ever paid extra for a checked bag and then again for a seat assignment, then you’ll love the new subscription tiers and models that allow you to feel in control of how you’re nickel and dimed into submission.

There’s even shrinkflation. Anthropic’s own documentation notes that the tokenizer introduced with Opus 4.7 can use up to 35 percent more tokens for the same text. The fare stays the same, and the legroom quietly shrinks an inch.

The buffet is closing

The clearest sign of the shift is the death of the flat rate. GitHub Copilot moved to usage-based billing in June. Cursor and Replit made similar moves. Consumer subscriptions picked up weekly limits and peak-hour throttling.

Higher ed got its notice too. On July 1, OpenAI moved ChatGPT Edu to a subscription-plus-usage model, and campus IT offices from UConn to Maryland spent the summer writing explainer pages about credit pools and license tiers. UC Davis accounts now come with 100 credits a week. Fordham requires training before handing out a pro license, and that training tells people to use cheaper models for routine work.

I’ll admit I’ve been a buffet customer. I built the same incremental game three separate times with three different AI tools, just to compare them. Nobody charged me per egg. I don’t expect that to last, and I suspect a lot of the faculty experimentation happening right now is running on the same assumption.

What this means for those of us teaching with AI

A few predictions, offered with the humility of someone who works in higher ed and has watched plenty of technology forecasts age poorly.

First, model choice becomes a literacy skill. Knowing when a task needs the front of the cabin and when basic economy will do is going to matter as much as knowing how to write a good prompt. Fordham is already teaching this. Most of our AI literacy frameworks don’t mention cost at all, and they should.

Second, campus licenses will start to look like travel budgets, and you’ll have to choose between that conference in the Caribbean and the enterprise account seat you now rely on. When usage is metered, someone has to decide who gets how many credits. That conversation will land in faculty senates and budget offices, and it will be awkward. A doctoral student running a big agentic literature review and an undergraduate asking for help outlining an essay do not cost the same, and soon the invoice will say so.

Third, the gap between institutions will widen. Wealthy campuses will buy premium seats for everyone. Smaller and under-resourced institutions will route more of their people to the cheap models, or to open-weight models they host themselves. That isn’t necessarily a disaster, since the cheap models are quite good now. It is an equity question, though, and we should be asking it out loud before the procurement contracts are signed.

Fourth, efficiency will become a pedagogical value. For three years the advice has been to try everything because it’s cheap. The new advice is closer to knowing what you’re asking for before you ask. Honestly, that is decent advice for doctoral students regardless of tokens.

We are now beginning our final descent

A descent isn’t a crash. Airlines are a hard business, and people still fly. Flight’s argument is that the steadiest profits may go to the companies that own the infrastructure, which are the airports in his metaphor, rather than to the labs. Whether or not he’s right, the labs now have shareholders asking every quarter how full the planes were.

For those of us in higher ed, it’s worth being honest about where we are in the flight. The early days of generous usage limits and a new free feature every few weeks were the cruising altitude, and we are on our way down from it. Expect tighter limits and more tiers, with fewer pleasant surprises along the way. The flat fare is gone, and every seat now has a price. Start building cost into how you teach AI, and into how your campus plans to pay for it.
