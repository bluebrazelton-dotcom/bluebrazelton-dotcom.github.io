Workshop

Citation Needed

One idea, an incremental game about an academic career, built three times with three different AI tools and three very different ways of working. All three builds are playable here, with notes on how each one was made.

I tried to build a video game and accidentally made three

Over the last year, I’ve tried to learn as much about generative AI as I can, and it has been an incredible journey of theory-to-practice, and from early September until mid-October that meant vibe coding an academic-career-themed incremental upgrade game. I built it first with ChatGPT and Codex over many sessions across a few weeks, then with Claude Code across just a few sessions, and then I asked Gemini for a one-shot. I vibe coded them in that order, losing more and more patience toward the end (hence the one-shot attempt, built on the info from the other two builds).

But let me introduce Citation Needed: an incremental upgrade game based on the academic career, complete with four disciplines (Social Scientist, Bench Scientist, Historian, and Legal Scholar). Technically, it is the third of three games that share one idea and not much else, and you can play all three here, each with its own page and the scope document I started from, so you can check my work.

One of my learning outcomes for myself was really about learning the difference between quality AI content and AI slop. What amount of effort does one have to put into a project to eliminate the sloppiness caused by probabilistic models making educated guesses about what the prompt was really asking for? AI slop is easy, fast, convenient, and most of all it is obvious. You see it everywhere you go; maybe even your local burger spot has an AI slop logo now.

But getting high-quality output? With fidelity to what you intended initially? This is effortful. And here is the part that took me three builds to understand: when that effort is done well, you never see it. The slop is the AI content you notice, because it is everywhere and it announces itself; the good AI content, the course page that simply made sense or the email that actually answered your question, you probably didn’t even notice, because someone put the time in to develop something effective long before it reached you.

The Gemini build named itself Citation Needed, and the name turned out to be the best lesson of the whole project (with a nod to the Wikipedia editors who have been hanging that little bracketed flag on confident claims for as long as I have been reading Wikipedia). A citation-needed claim sounds right, reads smoothly, and has nothing underneath it, which is now my working definition of slop: output that sounds finished when nobody has actually checked it, a much lower bar than ugly and a much more common one.

Build 1: six weeks and a lot of paperwork. The ChatGPT build was the careful one, or at least the one that looked careful, since before a single line of game code ChatGPT and I spent almost three weeks producing research and design documents about what “the top of academia” means, a twelve-chapter career arc, an economy model, and a risk register. By the end, the project folder held about 555,000 words of planning and review, roughly six novels about a game. The game itself is the most complete of the three (four careers, 48 chapters, 13 endings), and it is also not what I scoped, because the approved scope said one social-science career and stated outright that the base game “does not contain four fully separate versions” of it. It shipped four. The build passed 34,690 automated save tests, while the same records admit that “unfamiliar-player comprehension” was never measured, and when I finally played it myself, my note was that the writing “felt like a puzzle to decipher.” [citation needed]

I have to own my part in that, too. Somewhere in the middle of those six weeks, I found myself fixating on minute details like grammar and the number of clicks it took to finish a task (there is an entire research document in the folder about reducing click tedium), while the bigger problems with the game sat there untouched. It was like dialing in radio presets on a car that doesn’t run. The paperwork made that easy, because every small fix produced a satisfying new document confirming it had been fixed, and none of those documents ever asked whether anyone would want to play the thing.

Build 2: one page and a hard stop. I started the Claude Code build the same day Build 1 closed, and the first rule I wrote down was that nothing from the previous build’s folder was in scope. The whole brief fit on one page: it named four button types, set the joke rules (punch at situations, never people), and defined done as “a person plays ten minutes without instruction and wants chapter 2.” We balanced Chapter 1 in a spreadsheet and a small simulation before building anything, and the agent was told to stop and show me the first chapter before touching the second, which it did, politely and on schedule. It is the closest of the three to the invisible kind of good: small, plain, and almost nothing in it calls attention to itself, which is exactly what a page of rules and a hard stop bought me. It was also called The Patience Game, and that is about where I ran out of patience.

Build 3: one afternoon and a confident report. By the Gemini build I knew what I wanted, so I handed over what I had learned from the first two and asked for a one-shot. Three scoping questions later, I had a game that looked better in its first five minutes than anything the other two had produced, and about an hour after that I also had a development report, which praised its own careful scoping “rather than jumping blindly into code generation” and described my six-week ChatGPT build as a “single-shot” build. It was wrong about my own project, and it was wrong fluently. [citation needed]

Put side by side, the three builds taught me that slop comes in two flavors. Underspecify the work and you get confident filler, like the flashy game with the made-up report; overspecify it and you get bureaucratic filler, like thousands of tests and half a million words that never answered the one question that mattered, which is whether a person enjoys playing it. The model was not really the variable. The brief was, and so was my willingness to review and test what came back.

Honestly, none of the three builds are great, not compared to what I wanted, but ultimately, I’ve pushed as hard as I can with this side quest and need to move on to other things. I learned my lessons, and all it would take to get what I wanted is iteration. I had each model play the game it built, and the ones the other models built, because my ADHD was screaming every time I had to playtest. That helped, up to a point: one AI’s audit of another AI’s game was genuinely useful, and the second AI’s review of that audit found that some of it was wrong. The AI could write the game, the tests, and the report about the game, but it could not tell me whether the game was any good. That still took a person playing it.

Which brings me to what I actually want to say to my colleagues. We have spent the last few years talking about AI literacy in frameworks, competencies, workshops, and policy statements (I study institutional AI policy, so I have read more of these than anyone should). Stop talking about AI literacy and start developing it, because AI literacy is a hands-on subject. I did not learn the difference between slop and quality from a framework; I learned it by scoping a game too loosely, then scoping one too tightly, then watching a model confidently misdescribe my own work. My doctoral students learn it the same way: scoping a build is framing a research question, scope creep from one career to four is the dissertation that keeps adding chapters, and testing by playing instead of trusting the report is checking claims against the data.

I’m sure I could have built a better intake process. I know I could have learned more Python before I started. But I needed to try my hand at building more apps, and the learning was significant.

So here they are: three builds, three scope documents, and my notes on where each one went sideways. Play them, poke at them, and then go build something of your own, because that is the only way I know to learn the difference. And if something in here sounds right but has nothing underneath it, you know what to call it.
